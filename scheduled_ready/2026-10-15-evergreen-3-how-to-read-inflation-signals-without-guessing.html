Optimal Inflation Rate: A Meta-Analysis Source: arXivInflation is a complex economic phenomenon that has puzzled economists for decades. The optimal inflation rate, which is the level of inflation that maximizes long-term economic growth, has been a subject of much debate. This article revisits the optimal long-run inflation rate using 777 estimates from 116 primary studies published between 1989 and 2026, the largest sample on the topic to date. The research aims to provide a comprehensive analysis of the optimal inflation rate and its implications for monetary policy.

The optimal inflation rate is estimated to be about 0.6 percentage points per year, well below the two-percent targets used by most advanced-economy central banks. This finding suggests that the two-percent inflation target may not be the best approach for all economies. The gap between the optimal inflation rate and the two-percent target should not be automatically read as a verdict against the two-percent norm. Measurement error in published price indices could close, widen, or even reverse the gap, and the structural literature itself cannot pin down the sign of the required correction.

Bayesian model averaging over the full set of structural moderators shows that cross-study variation is driven by real modelling choices rather than by selective reporting. The main drivers are the choice of monetary benchmark (Friedman rule vs. laissez-faire), the transactions-frictions technology, the assumed shock structure, and the class of nominal-rigidity contract. The non-parametric caliper test finds no upward bunching at the two-percent target, indicating that the two-percent target is not a fixed point in the literature.

The research contributes a reproducible LLM-assisted extraction pipeline for structurally calibrated literature and a quantitative decomposition of where the optimal-inflation literature disagrees. This pipeline allows for a more accurate and reliable analysis of the optimal inflation rate, providing valuable insights into the factors that influence the optimal inflation rate. Inflation, currency substitution, and dollarization

Understanding Inflation, Currency Substitution, and Dollarization in Argentina Source: the DOI recordInflation, currency substitution, and dollarization have been pivotal issues in Argentina's economic history, particularly in the context of the country's economic struggles and political instability. This section delves into the complexities of these phenomena, focusing on the role of inflation, the impact of currency substitution, and the implications of dollarization. The Role of Inflation When consumers in Argentina are limited to holding only domestic monetary balances, an increase in the rate of inflation leads to a balance of payments deficit. This is because the purchasing power of domestic currency decreases, making it less attractive for foreign buyers.

Consequently, the country's foreign exchange reserves decrease, leading to a deficit in the balance of payments. This deficit can be financed through foreign borrowing, which can lead to further inflationary pressures if not managed properly. Currency Substitution and Its Heterogeneity Currency substitution refers to the practice of holding different currencies simultaneously, often in a country where multiple currencies are used. This practice can be driven by non-proportional lump-sum transfers, where individuals hold different currencies for specific purposes.

For instance, some individuals may hold US dollars for their foreign investments, while others hold euros for their European business activities. This heterogeneity can lead to different inflationary pressures depending on the relative stability of each currency. Imperfect Substitutability and Its Implications The concept of imperfect substitutability between currencies suggests that while individuals may hold different currencies, they do not necessarily substitute one for the other. This means that the inflationary effects of one currency do not automatically translate to the other.

However, this does not necessarily mean that there is no scope for rational (hyper)inflationary processes. The parametrization used in the model determines the extent to which these effects can be mitigated. Therefore, the outcome of inflationary processes is not solely determined by the intrinsic characteristics of imperfect substitutability but by the specific parameters used in the model. Dollarization and Its Limitations Dollarization is the practice of using the US dollar as the primary currency in a country.

This practice can lead to currency substitution, as individuals may hold US dollars for various reasons, such as investments or international transactions.

How to Read Inflation Signals Without Guessing Source: the DOI recordInflation is a complex economic phenomenon that can be challenging to predict and understand. However, by closely examining the historical context and the reasons behind the adoption of inflation targeting in Japan, we can gain valuable insights into how to read inflation signals without relying on guesswork. The Bank of Japan's decision to not adopt inflation targeting, despite the growing interest in the concept, is a prime example of this. The Bank of Japan's stance against inflation targeting can be explained through the lens of political economy.

In the early years, the Board members were uncertain about the appropriate price index and the specific inflation rate they should aim for. A Bank of Japan study completed in October 2000 did not provide clear answers, leaving them with a lack of confidence in the effectiveness of inflation targeting. This uncertainty led to the decision not to adopt inflation targeting in the early years. However, as the interest in inflation targeting grew, the Board members began to adopt a more inflation-targeting-like commitment strategy in March 2001.

Despite this, the adoption of inflation targeting was delayed due to concerns about the availability of conventional tools to increase the inflation rate. The Board members believed that announcing a target with a positive inflation rate would damage confidence, as it could be perceived as a commitment to a higher inflation rate. This concern was further exacerbated by the Bank of Japan's worry about the transmission channels and the potential damage to its balance sheet. Towards the end of Governor Hayami's term, the views against inflation targeting turned sharply negative.

News reports suggested that the new Governor's appointment was linked to the adoption of inflation targeting. This shift in opinion led to the adoption of inflation targeting in later years, despite the initial reluctance. The decision to not adopt inflation targeting in Japan can be understood from a political economy perspective. The uncertainty about the appropriate price index and the specific inflation rate, combined with the lack of confidence in conventional tools to increase the inflation rate, led to the initial hesitation.
