Mortgage Design in an Equilibrium Model of the Housing Market

### Mortgage Design in an Equilibrium Model of the Housing Market National Bureau of Economic Research In a quantitative equilibrium life cycle model with aggregate shocks, long-term mortgages, and an equilibrium housing market, mortgage design plays a crucial role in reducing housing market volatility, consumption volatility, and default. The model focuses on designs that index payments to monetary policy. Designs that raise mortgage payments in booms and lower them in recessions perform better than designs with fixed mortgage payments. The benefits are quantitatively substantial: In a simulated crisis under a monetary regime in which the central bank lowers real interest rates in a bust, house prices fall 2.24 percentage points less, 23 percent fewer households default, and consumption falls by 0.79 percentage points less with ARMs relative to FRMs.

Among designs that reduce payments in a bust, front-loading the payment reductions and concentrating them in recessions outperforms designs that spread payment reductions over the life of the mortgage. Front-loading alleviates household liquidity constraints in states where they are most binding, reducing default and stimulating housing demand by new homeowners. To isolate this channel, the model compares an FRM with a built-in option to be converted to an ARM with an FRM with an option to be refinanced at the prevailing FRM rate. Under these two contracts, the present value of a lender's loan falls by roughly an equal amount, but the FRM that can be converted to an ARM, which front loads payment reductions, reduces the declines in prices and consumption six times as much, and reduces default three times as much. Second Chances: Subprime Mortgage Modification and Redefault

Declining loan amounts push new mortgage redeliveries, but not all modifications lead to success. A recent study in the Journal of Money, Credit, and Banking reveals that mortgage modifications, particularly those involving subprime borrowers, significantly increase the likelihood of redeliveries over the following year. The average redefault rate for modifications that were pre-HAMP (Home Affordable Modification Program) and involved a significant reduction in monthly payments is 56%. This rate decreases as the magnitude of the payment reduction increases, and it further decreases when the reduction is achieved through principal forgiveness rather than lower interest rates. Journal of money credit and banking

The study, which focuses on modifications where the borrower was seriously delinquent and the monthly payment was reduced, suggests that modifications that involve a substantial reduction in monthly payments are more effective at preventing redeliveries. This finding is particularly significant for borrowers who are struggling to make their mortgage payments, as it provides a clear pathway for them to avoid defaulting on their loans. Journal of money credit and banking

However, the study also highlights the importance of the mechanism used in the modification. While a reduction in monthly payments is generally more effective, modifications that involve principal forgiveness are even more effective in preventing redeliveries. This is likely due to the fact that principal forgiveness allows borrowers to pay off a portion of their debt, which can make it easier for them to meet their monthly obligations. The study's findings have important implications for both borrowers and lenders. For borrowers, it provides a clear strategy for avoiding redeliveries and maintaining their creditworthiness. For lenders, it suggests that modifications that involve principal forgiveness are more effective at preventing redeliveries and can help to maintain their reputation and reduce the risk of default. While the study provides valuable insights into the effectiveness of mortgage modifications, it is important to note that the results may not be applicable to all borrowers or all modifications. The study's findings are based on modifications that were pre-HAMP and involved a significant reduction in monthly payments, and it is possible that modifications that involve other mechanisms or borrowers who are not delinquent may have different results.
