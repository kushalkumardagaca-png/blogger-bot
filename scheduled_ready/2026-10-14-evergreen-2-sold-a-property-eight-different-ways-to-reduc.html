Capital gains and surplus stripping

### Section 7: Capital Gains and Surplus Stripping documented source evidence Capital gains and surplus stripping are complex tax avoidance mechanisms that have been increasingly scrutinized in recent years. These practices involve the manipulation of corporate earnings and dividends to minimize tax liabilities, particularly focusing on the second level of tax on corporate source income. This second level of tax, known as capital gains tax, is often levied on the appreciation of capital assets, such as stocks and real estate, held by corporations. documented source evidence The concept of surplus stripping, or dividend-stripping, has been a longstanding practice in Canada since the 1926 revision of the Income War Tax Act, 1917.

This scheme was introduced to tax corporations and their shareholders, aiming to address the double taxation of corporate earnings. As a result of changes to the Income Tax Act in 1977, the scope of surplus stripping operations was greatly limited, and subsequent legislation moved to block the few remaining perceived abuses. documented source evidence Capital gains strips or bailouts represent a much less long-standing problem for fiscal authorities. These mechanisms allow individuals to defer capital gains tax upon the realization of appreciated capital property, effectively deferring tax on the appreciation of their assets.

This practice has been criticized for undermining the principle of equity, a cornerstone of tax reform. While capital gains strips may not precipitate a serious loss of revenue, they undermine the principle of equity, a cornerstone of tax reform. documented source evidence Surplus stripping and capital gains strips are mechanisms that have been curtailed, primarily by the elimination of the incentive to engage in them. The government has only recently moved to eradicate capital gains avoidance with complicated and as yet untested legislation.

The present study deals with both types of avoidance activities mentioned in the preceding paragraphs. The first chapter traces the evolution of dividend-stripping in Canada from its beginnings to the time of the enactment of the most recent legislative provisions designed to combat it. These provisions are analyzed and commented on in the second and third chapters of the thesis. The fourth and fifth chapters present an interjurisdictional comparison, investigating surplus stripping activities and the corresponding efforts of the fiscal authorities to combat them in Great Britain and the United States.

### Limited Interests in Property: Overview and Taxation documented source evidence Limited interests in property refer to the rights and limitations that individuals or trusts have over certain types of property, particularly in the context of capital gains taxation. This concept is crucial for understanding how property transactions are taxed, especially in the context of capital gains and losses. documented source evidence The taxation of limited interests in property is governed by the Internal Revenue Code, which divides taxable gains and deductible losses into two classes: ordinary and capital. Broadly speaking, capital gains are taxed at preferential, lower rates, whereas ordinary losses can be deducted from taxable income under certain circumstances.

However, the treatment of capital losses is more complex, as they are only deductible from a limited segment of income and may be carried forward indefinitely. documented source evidence The Treasury's interest in maximizing revenue conflicts with taxpayers' desire to minimize taxes, leading to a controversy over the "capital" versus "ordinary" dichotomy. The Code effects the division between capital and ordinary gain or loss by prescribing "capital" treatment for the gains and losses arising on the "sale or exchange" of "capital assets," which are described as a limited class of property. This requires a particular kind of transaction in a particular type of property to qualify for capital treatment.

documented source evidence The "sale or exchange" requirement can be interpreted in relation to sections 1001, which outlines the method of computing gain or loss on the "sale or other disposition of property," and section 61(a)(3), which brings "gains derived from dealings in property" into gross income. These provisions apply only to transactions that "close out" the taxpayer's ownership interest in the property involved. Capital assets are defined as property held by the taxpayer that does not fit within five exclusionary categories, such as personal use, family use, or items of insignificant value. documented source evidence In summary, limited interests in property are subject to taxation based on their classification as capital or ordinary gains or losses.

The distinction between these categories is crucial for understanding the tax treatment of property transactions and the potential for capital gains or losses. The complexity of this classification system reflects the Treasury's interest in maximizing revenue and the taxpayers' desire to minimize taxes, leading to ongoing controversy and debate over the "capital" versus "ordinary" dichotomy.

### Section 7: Capital Gains and Surplus Stripping [[S7|documented source evidence]] Capital gains and surplus stripping are complex tax avoidance mechanisms that have been increasingly scrutinized in recent years. These practices involve the manipulation of corporate earnings and dividends to minimize tax liabilities, particularly focusing on the second level of tax on corporate source income. This second level of tax, known as capital gains tax, is often levied on the appreciation of capital assets, such as stocks and real estate, held by corporations. [[S7|documented source evidence]] The concept of surplus stripping, or dividend-stripping, has been a longstanding practice in Canada since the 1926 revision of the Income War Tax Act, 1917. This scheme was introduced to tax corporations and their shareholders, aiming to address the double taxation of corporate earnings. As a result of changes to the Income Tax Act in 1977, the scope of surplus stripping operations was greatly limited, and subsequent legislation moved to block the few remaining perceived abuses. [[S7|documented source evidence]] Capital gains strips or bailouts represent a much less long-standing problem for fiscal authorities. These mechanisms allow individuals to defer capital gains tax upon the realization of appreciated capital property, effectively deferring tax on the appreciation of their assets. This practice has been criticized for undermining the principle of equity, a cornerstone of tax reform. While capital gains strips may not precipitate a serious loss of revenue, they undermine the principle of equity, a cornerstone of tax reform. [[S7|documented source evidence]] Surplus stripping and capital gains strips are mechanisms that have been curtailed, primarily by the elimination of the incentive to engage in them. The government has only recently moved to eradicate capital gains avoidance with complicated and as yet untested legislation. The present study deals with both types of avoidance activities mentioned in the preceding paragraphs. The first chapter traces the evolution of dividend-stripping in Canada from its beginnings to the time of the enactment of the most recent legislative provisions designed to combat it. These provisions are analyzed and commented on in the second and third chapters of the thesis. The fourth and fifth chapters present an interjurisdictional comparison, investigating surplus stripping activities and the corresponding efforts of the fiscal authorities to combat them in Great Britain and the United States.
