Saving the Farm or Giving Away the Farm: A Critical Analysis of the Capital Gains Tax Preferences Source: University of San DiegoThe capital gains tax, a cornerstone of the federal income tax system, has long been a subject of heated debate. Critics argue that it disproportionately benefits the wealthy, while proponents claim it helps stimulate the economy. This article delves into the complexities of the capital gains tax, focusing on its impact on individual income taxes and its implications for estate planning. The capital gains tax, as applied to individual income taxes, has been criticized for its questionable effect on the economy.

However, the primary reason it has not generated more revenue is due to tax policy. Congress has created and continues to create policies that encourage holding capital property for long periods, thereby delaying or denying revenue to the federal government. This mechanism shifts wealth to the wealthiest taxpayers, exacerbating income inequality. Another example of gross inequities is revealed in the income tax implications of the capital gains tax when property passes through an estate.

When capital gains property is transferred through an estate, the inherent gain is completely eradicated. This practice, known as "phantom income," is a significant issue that affects the middle and lower classes, who often inherit property and face this tax burden. This paper proposes reforms to address some of the inequities in tax policy. The proposals are specifically designed to reallocate capital gains preferences to shift certain benefits towards the middle and lower class, phase out certain preferences, and eliminate preferences that only benefit the wealthiest taxpayers.

The goal is to create a more balanced approach to tax policy, moving away from the current system that favors the wealthy. One of the key proposals is to eliminate the preferential treatment of capital gains under the present system. This would eliminate a "loophole" that substantially benefits high-income individuals. Additionally, the separation of the stockholder and the corporation as a taxable entity should fall with the demise of capital gains.

This reform would simplify the tax system and eliminate many of the inequities that arise from the current system. The article also highlights the complexity and inequities in corporate taxation, particularly in the separation of the stockholder and the corporation.

Limited Interests in Property: An Overview Source: the DOI recordLimited interests in property refer to the rights and interests that individuals or entities have in a property, typically excluding the right to use or benefit from the property. These interests are often subject to restrictions or limitations that may affect their value and tax implications. Understanding these interests is crucial for individuals and trusts, as well as for the taxation authorities, particularly in relation to capital gains tax. The taxation of limited interests in property is a complex area that has been the subject of much debate and research.

The Internal Revenue Code (IRC) divides taxable gains and deductible losses into two classes: ordinary and capital. Broadly speaking, capital gains are taxed at preferential, lower rates, while ordinary losses can only be deducted from taxable income under certain circumstances. Capital losses are deductible from only a limited segment of income, but may be carried forward indefinitely. The favorable consequences of capital gain taxation and the mixed consequences of capital loss treatment have led taxpayers to seek "capital" or "ordinary" treatment as their interests dictate.

The Treasury's interest in maximizing revenue conflicts with the taxpayers' desire to minimize taxes, leading to a controversy that has placed much pressure on the "capital"-"ordinary" dichotomy. The Code effects the division between capital and ordinary gain or loss by prescribing "capital" treatment for the gains and losses arising on the "sale or exchange" of "capital assets," which are described as a limited class of property. This requires a particular kind of transaction in a particular type of property to qualify for capital treatment. The "sale or exchange" requirement can be interpreted in relation to sections 1001, which outlines the method of computing gain or loss on the "sale or other disposition of property," and 61(a)(3), which brings "gains derived from dealings in property" into gross income.

These provisions apply only to transactions that "close out" the taxpayer's ownership interest in the property involved. Capital assets are defined as property held by the taxpayer that does not fit within five exclusionary categories. By and large, the exclusionary categories include items like collectibles, personal property, and certain types of investments. The exclusion of these items from the definition of capital assets limits the scope of property that can be subject to capital gains tax treatment.

HOW SHOULD CAPITAL BE TAXED?

How Should Capital Be Taxed? Capital taxation is a crucial aspect of an optimal tax system, but not all ways of taxing capital income, wealth, property, inheritances, and corporate profits strike a balance between optimality and administrative feasibility. The optimal tax system aims to maximize tax revenue while minimizing distortions and administrative complexity. This survey discusses the theoretical optimal tax literature, empirical studies on the distribution of capital and the distortionary costs of capital taxation, and analyzes the desirability of specific taxes on capital income, wealth, property, inheritances, and corporate profits.

Theoretical optimal tax literature suggests that capital taxation plays a significant role in an optimal tax system, but only certain ways of taxing capital are able to strike a balance between optimality and administrative feasibility. For instance, the 28% rate on capital gains was adopted by the Republican administration under President Bush from 1989 through 1992 without raising the individual rate on ordinary income, which had the effect of restoring a capital gains preference at that rate, but limited only to individuals. This approach was unsuccessful, as Congress held hearings on and debated capital gains in 1989 and 1990, and raised the top individual permanent rate on ordinary income to 31% in 1990, while maintaining the 28% ceiling on capital gains! The distribution of capital is a critical factor in determining the optimal tax system.

Empirical studies show that capital is not evenly distributed across different income groups, with the top 1% of income earners holding a significant portion of the capital. This uneven distribution of capital can lead to distortions in the economy, such as reduced investment and higher interest rates. Therefore, a tax system that targets the distribution of capital can help reduce these distortions and promote economic efficiency. The distortionary costs of capital taxation are another important consideration in determining the optimal tax system.

The distortionary costs of capital taxation include deadweight loss, tax evasion, and tax avoidance. Deadweight loss occurs when the tax system reduces the quantity of a good or service that is produced and consumed, leading to a loss of economic efficiency. Tax evasion and tax avoidance are also distortionary costs, as they reduce the amount of tax revenue collected by the government.

Limited Interests in Property: An Overview [[S1|]] Limited interests in property refer to the rights and interests that individuals or entities have in a property, typically excluding the right to use or benefit from the property. These interests are often subject to restrictions or limitations that may affect their value and tax implications. Understanding these interests is crucial for individuals and trusts, as well as for the taxation authorities, particularly in relation to capital gains tax. [[S1|]] The taxation of limited interests in property is a complex area that has been the subject of much debate and research. The Internal Revenue Code (IRC) divides taxable gains and deductible losses into two classes: ordinary and capital. Broadly speaking, capital gains are taxed at preferential, lower rates, while ordinary losses can only be deducted from taxable income under certain circumstances. Capital losses are deductible from only a limited segment of income, but may be carried forward indefinitely. The favorable consequences of capital gain taxation and the mixed consequences of capital loss treatment have led taxpayers to seek "capital" or "ordinary" treatment as their interests dictate. [[S1|]] The Treasury's interest in maximizing revenue conflicts with the taxpayers' desire to minimize taxes, leading to a controversy that has placed much pressure on the "capital"-"ordinary" dichotomy. The Code effects the division between capital and ordinary gain or loss by prescribing "capital" treatment for the gains and losses arising on the "sale or exchange" of "capital assets," which are described as a limited class of property. This requires a particular kind of transaction in a particular type of property to qualify for capital treatment. The "sale or exchange" requirement can be interpreted in relation to sections 1001, which outlines the method of computing gain or loss on the "sale or other disposition of property," and 61(a)(3), which brings "gains derived from dealings in property" into gross income. These provisions apply only to transactions that "close out" the taxpayer's ownership interest in the property involved. [[S1|]] Capital assets are defined as property held by the taxpayer that does not fit within five exclusionary categories. By and large, the exclusionary categories include items like collectibles, personal property, and certain types of investments. The exclusion of these items from the definition of capital assets limits the scope of property that can be subject to capital gains tax treatment.
