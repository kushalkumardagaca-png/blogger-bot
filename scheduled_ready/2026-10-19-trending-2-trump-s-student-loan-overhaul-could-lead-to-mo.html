The article, authored by scholars at Southern Methodist University Law Review, contends that the requirement that student loans be discharged only under specific conditions and procedures is a significant barrier to achieving the goals of student loans, which include promoting equality of access to higher education, educating the population for the benefit of the country, enabling students' free choice of career, and providing a benefit to students. SMU Law Review The article draws on the legislative history of the student-loan programs to establish that they serve at least four distinct purposes: providing equality of access to higher education, educating the population for the benefit of the country, enabling students' free choice of career, and providing a benefit to students. It then examines the empirical literature and fundamental principles of bankruptcy law to demonstrate that nondischargeability can thwart these purposes through four different effects: deterring students from higher education, distorting career choice, discouraging borrowers from economic and social participation, and rendering student loans harmful to borrowers.

SMU Law Review The article proposes that nondischargeability should be applied narrowly, only in situations where its goals are advanced without undue interference with other goals of the programs. It suggests ideas for tempering each of the four negative effects. To avoid deterring education, the article recommends that the fact that bankruptcy relief often can be had if requested should be made more salient. To combat distortion of career choice, bankruptcy courts should stop ruling that debtors should abandon lower-paying jobs for which their education has prepared them.

To mitigate borrower discouragement and harm, courts and the Department of Education should take account of the likelihood of these effects in deciding on discharge. The debt-income ratio may be a proxy for discouragement, and inability to find a job in one's field may be a proxy for harm.
