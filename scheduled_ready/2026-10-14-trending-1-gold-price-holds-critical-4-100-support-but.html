Inflation is a situation where the general level of prices for goods and services is rising, leading to a decrease in the purchasing power of money. As inflation rises, the purchasing power of gold, which is a store of value, increases. This makes gold a more attractive investment option for those looking to protect their wealth from inflation. The Federal Reserve's monetary policy has also played a crucial role in the gold market.

The Federal Reserve, the central bank of the United States, has been implementing monetary policies that have been aimed at controlling inflation. These policies, such as reducing the money supply and increasing interest rates, have had a significant impact on the gold market. When interest rates rise, the demand for gold decreases as it becomes more expensive to hold. This is because gold is a non-interest-bearing asset, and investors prefer to hold assets that offer a higher return.

This is because gold is a safe-haven asset, and investors often turn to gold when they are concerned about the stability of the U.S. dollar. This paper investigates the extent to which the increase in oil prices is due to excessive speculation in the oil futures market.

The analysis suggests that excessive speculation has been responsible for 24%-48% of the increase in the WTI crude oil price during October 2020-June 2022. These estimates translate into an oil price increase of around $18-$36 per barrel and an increase in the U.S. PCE inflation rate by circa 0.75-1.5% points during the same period. The paper complements the analysis with an empirical investigation of the crude oil market, which shows that speculative long noncommercial open-interest positions in oil futures have increased considerably relative to short noncommercial positions.

This increase in speculative positions has led to higher futures prices for crude oil, which in turn "Granger-causes" oil spot prices, the futures prices of corn and soybeans, and the fertilizer price. These econometric results indicate that oil speculators not only raise oil prices but also drive up food commodity prices. This underscores the systemic adverse consequences of excessive speculation in the oil market for the U.S. economy.

These econometric results indicate that oil speculators not only raise oil prices but also drive up food commodity prices. This underscores the systemic adverse consequences of excessive speculation in the oil market for the U.S. economy.
