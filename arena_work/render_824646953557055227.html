The FIRE Number Is a Scenario, Not a Finish Line

Financial independence is often compressed into one equation: annual spending multiplied by a fixed number. That shortcut is useful for orientation, but it is not a retirement plan. The result changes with taxes, health coverage, irregular costs, withdrawal horizon, inflation, market sequence, later income and the amount of spending that can actually adjust.

A household spending £40,000 and dividing by 4% gets £1 million. Dividing the same spending by 3.5% produces about £1.14 million; at 3%, about £1.33 million. None of those figures is automatically safe. Each embeds assumptions about portfolio, duration, future returns, inflation, flexibility and the acceptable chance of changing course.

Early retirement extends the period that savings may need to support. It can also create years before public retirement benefits, public health eligibility or unrestricted access to particular retirement accounts. The plan therefore needs at least three clocks: years until work ends, bridge years before later resources begin, and the full lifetime horizon.

The U.S. Department of Labor says no retirement-income rule of thumb fits everyone because expenses, medical costs, debt and the desired lifestyle differ. Its retirement guide recommends estimating income, savings and likely expenses rather than relying on a universal replacement percentage. FIRE requires the same discipline, only over a potentially longer span.

Do not treat a portfolio milestone as permission to resign immediately. A go/no-go decision also needs cash reserves, verified insurance, a withdrawal route, tax estimates, contingency capacity and agreement among household members. One number can identify a review point. It cannot prove that every operational dependency is ready.

This article uses pound-denominated illustrations, while several authoritative sources describe U.S. systems. Their principles—expense tracking, longevity, healthcare bridges, benefit timing and account access—are broadly useful, but legal, tax and benefit details are jurisdiction-specific. Replace every example with current local rules and qualified advice where needed.

Define FIRE in practical terms. Does it mean never earning again, leaving one career, reducing hours, changing to lower-paid meaningful work or gaining the ability to refuse bad conditions? A plan that permits optional income has different stress capacity from one that prohibits all future earnings. The label should not hide the operating model.

The durable target is not the largest portfolio screenshot. It is a funded lifetime system that can pay essential expenses, absorb plausible shocks and adjust discretionary spending without destroying the reason for retiring. That system needs several numbers, written decisions and recurring tests.

Start with a range rather than a point: minimum launch balance, preferred balance, required cash reserve and spending guardrails. Ranges acknowledge uncertainty and prevent a small market move on a chosen date from pretending to decide whether a multi-decade life plan succeeds.

Calculate targets with both current full spending and the higher of recent inflation-adjusted spending. If the launch case works only after aggressive cuts not yet tested in real life, run a six-month practice budget while income still provides recovery room.

Build Spending from the Ledger Up

Use at least twelve months of actual transactions and add costs that appear less frequently. Categorize spending as essential, committed but changeable, discretionary and irregular. The categories serve a response plan: when markets disappoint, the household knows what can pause without pretending rent, food or insurance are flexible.

Start with current spending, then transform it for life after work. Remove genuine work costs only after verifying they disappear; add travel, hobbies, equipment, professional dues, home time, dependent support and other costs that may rise. Retirement is not automatically cheaper merely because commuting ends.

Annualize irregular expenses through sinking funds. Roofs, vehicles, dental work, devices, family travel, legal fees and appliance replacement are not emergencies simply because they do not occur monthly. Estimate useful lives, replacement ranges and timing, then include annual funding rather than waiting for a large withdrawal to surprise the model.

Separate housing into mortgage or rent, taxes, insurance, maintenance, utilities and accessibility changes. A paid-off mortgage does not make housing free. Moving can lower one cost while adding transaction expenses, renovation, association charges or a different tax burden. Use the intended location and realistic tenure.

Model taxes as an expense generated by the withdrawal plan, not a percentage copied from salary. Different account types, gains, dividends, pensions and conversions can produce different taxable income. Include local tax and benefit interactions. A £40,000 lifestyle may require more than £40,000 of gross withdrawals.

Healthcare needs premiums, deductibles, copayments, prescriptions, dental, vision and uncovered care. BLS research found healthcare spending generally rises with age and that insurance premiums and drugs were important components for older households. The expenditure evidence does not predict one family, but it argues against assuming health costs remain flat.

Create a floor budget and a full-life budget. The floor covers housing, food, utilities, basic transport, insurance, tax and essential care. The full budget adds travel, gifts, dining, projects and other priorities. Withdrawal flexibility is credible only in the gap between those budgets; essential bills cannot be cut because a simulation says so.

Add a shock register with amount, probable timing and funding source. Include major repair, temporary family support, uninsured care, relocation and legal or tax work. Do not add every shock at maximum simultaneously, but stress combinations that have occurred in the household or could reasonably coincide.

Update spending for inflation by category rather than assuming every item follows one index perfectly. Healthcare, housing, tuition and travel can move differently. The model can use a general rate for planning, but the annual review should replace forecasts with actual category changes.

Include support flowing both directions across generations. Adult children, parents and other relatives can create cash needs that do not appear in personal-category averages. Agree on boundaries and build only commitments the household can sustain through weak markets.

A Withdrawal Rate Must Name Its Horizon

A withdrawal rate is incomplete without a start date, horizon, portfolio, spending update rule, tax treatment and success definition. A 30-year historical study does not automatically answer a 50-year early-retirement question. Extending the horizon increases the range of inflation, market and personal outcomes the plan must survive.

Distinguish an initial withdrawal rate from a percentage-of-current-balance rule. Under an initial-rate method, the first withdrawal is based on starting assets and later amounts may rise with inflation. Under a current-balance method, spending changes directly with portfolio value. The cash-flow experience can be dramatically different.

Current research also changes as valuation, yields and assumptions change. Morningstar's 2025 analysis discussed a 3.7% starting rate for a 30-year, inflation-adjusted, fixed real-spending framework under its assumptions, while flexible methods supported different starting rates with more spending variability. The research discussion is evidence against treating 4% as permanent law.

Do not mix a low withdrawal rate with expenses that were understated. A cautious 3% applied to £30,000 is not safer if real recurring costs are £45,000. Verify numerator first, then test denominator. Precision in the percentage cannot repair missing healthcare, tax or replacement spending.

Define success beyond a positive final pound. The household may require a minimum reserve, legacy, housing asset, care fund or survivor income. A simulation that finishes with £1 at age 95 technically avoids depletion but may violate every practical margin along the way.

Use nominal and real views consistently. If returns are nominal, inflate future spending; if returns are real, keep purchasing-power spending constant where appropriate. Do not subtract inflation twice or compare a future nominal benefit with today's expense budget without converting one side.

Stress longevity for both partners rather than using an average life expectancy as a deadline. Investor.gov describes longevity risk as the risk of outliving assets and notes that lifetime annuity income can protect against it. That framing is useful even when an annuity is not chosen: the plan must address a long surviving life.

Run several horizons and identify what changes the decision. If 35 years looks robust but 50 years fails, options include working longer, saving more, lowering full spending, adding flexible income or revising legacy. The correct response is not to choose the shorter horizon because it gives the desired answer.

Record the withdrawal rule in plain language: first-year portfolio draw, inflation adjustment, permitted cuts and raises, review date, floor, ceiling and response to tax or benefit changes. A number without a rule cannot guide the second year.

Use sensitivity tables rather than debating one perfect assumption. Change spending, inflation, return, retirement date and later income one at a time, then combine unfavorable inputs. The variables that move the decision most deserve stronger evidence and wider safety margins.

Sequence Risk Arrives Before Average Return

Two retirees can earn the same long-run average return and experience different outcomes when the order differs. Losses early in retirement combine with withdrawals, leaving fewer assets to participate in recovery. Vanguard describes this as sequence-of-returns risk and notes that poor early returns can permanently impair the portfolio. Its retirement-income principles emphasize actions and flexibility that remain under the retiree's control.

Stress the first five and ten years separately. Apply a market decline, high inflation, a weak recovery and a large irregular expense while withdrawals continue. The scenario is not a forecast. It reveals which promises rely on favorable early markets and whether the contingency rules activate soon enough.

Hold a planned spending fund for near-term withdrawals if it supports behavior and execution, but count its opportunity cost. Cash can reduce forced selling and make bills predictable, while too much cash can lower expected growth and lose purchasing power. Choose a target tied to planned withdrawals, not fear.

Segment essential and discretionary spending before applying guardrails. A 10% portfolio-spending cut may be feasible when travel and projects form a large share, but impossible when nearly everything is housing, food and healthcare. Flexibility must be measured in pounds, not described as a personality trait.

A simple guardrail can pause inflation increases after a down year, direct optional income to the portfolio, or reduce discretionary spending when withdrawals exceed a stated percentage of current assets. The rule should also permit increases after strong outcomes, otherwise excessive underspending can become the hidden failure.

Dynamic spending does not create free income. Morningstar and Vanguard research show a trade-off: flexible methods can support different starting withdrawals, but year-to-year cash flow varies. The household must accept actual cuts rather than choosing a high flexible rate and later refusing the flexibility that justified it.

Avoid overreacting to one bad month or headline. Review on the schedule in the policy unless a true trigger occurs. Frequent changes invite market timing and convert a retirement-income system into a trading strategy. A guardrail is a formula, not permission to improvise whenever markets feel uncomfortable.

Test recovery actions in order: reduce discretionary spending, delay a project, use designated cash, earn optional income, rebalance under policy and consider structural changes. Selling growth assets after a decline solely from panic can lock in the outcome the sequence plan was designed to manage.

Track the funded ratio each year: current investable assets divided by the updated amount required under the same model. Also track floor coverage and years until later income. Portfolio return alone cannot show whether the lifetime plan improved.

Diversify the sources used for early withdrawals. A reserve, short-duration assets and a growth portfolio can serve different horizons, but buckets do not remove total-portfolio risk. Reconcile them back to one asset allocation and one spending rule.

Price the Bridge Before Public Benefits Begin

Someone leaving work at 45 may face roughly twenty years before age-65 health eligibility in the United States, seventeen years before Social Security can begin at 62, and more than fourteen years before the common age-59½ retirement-account threshold. These are separate bridges with separate rules, not one generic waiting period.

HealthCare.gov states that someone retiring before 65 who loses job-based coverage can use the Marketplace and that loss of coverage creates a Special Enrollment Period. Its retiree guidance also says premium tax credits and cost reductions depend on income and household size. That makes coverage and taxable-income planning connected decisions.

Obtain real insurance quotes for the retirement location and household. Compare premiums, deductible, out-of-pocket maximum, network, prescriptions and provider access. Test full-price coverage as well as any estimated assistance, because policy and income can change. The model needs both ordinary expected cost and a high-use year.

Do not count Social Security or another public benefit from the retirement date if eligibility begins later. SSA calculators compare estimates at 62, full retirement age and 70 and allow different future earnings assumptions. The official tools help replace a generic benefit guess with a personalized estimate from the actual earnings record.

Early work cessation can affect benefit estimates because future earnings stop. Enter the intended stop-work age rather than assuming decades of continued salary. Check survivor and spouse benefits where applicable, and retain a conservative case for legislative, employment-record or claiming uncertainty.

Map pensions, deferred compensation and employer retiree coverage by start date, inflation treatment, survivor election and counterparty. A nominal pension may lose purchasing power. A benefit with a strong survivor option may pay less initially but protect a spouse. The headline monthly amount is only one contract term.

Create a bridge-year table showing essential spending, full spending, healthcare, tax, portfolio withdrawal and each outside income source. The portfolio burden may be highest before public benefits begin and lower later. One perpetual withdrawal percentage can hide this changing shape.

Keep enough liquid access for the bridge without making the entire portfolio cash. Investor.gov notes that defined-contribution participants bear investment risk and must ensure savings last, while lifetime income can address longevity risk. Its lifetime-income guide supports separating guaranteed or later income from the portfolio draw.

If moving countries or regions is part of FIRE, verify residency, health access, currency, visa, tax and benefit rules before counting cheaper living costs. A travel budget is not a substitute for legal residence or durable medical access. Price a return-home scenario as well.

Model the bridge monthly for the first two years, then annually. Insurance enrollment, final salary, accrued leave, bonuses, taxes and benefit start dates rarely align neatly with January. A coarse annual model can show adequate cash while hiding a short first-year gap.

Optional Income Is a Risk Control, Not a Failure

FIRE culture can treat any paid work as evidence the plan failed. That framing removes one of the strongest controls available. A modest amount earned during weak early markets can reduce withdrawals, preserve assets and maintain skills without recreating the career the person left.

Model income by reliability. Contract work already arranged is different from a hope of consulting during a recession. Use zero in the base case unless the work is genuinely dependable, then show optional income as an upside or contingency. Never make retirement depend on employment the retiree may be unable to obtain.

Include taxes, equipment, insurance, unpaid time and business expenses. £15,000 of gross freelance revenue does not reduce portfolio withdrawals by £15,000 if costs and tax consume part of it. Estimate net cash and the timing of receipts.

Part-time work can also affect health coverage, public benefits, tax credits and pension calculations. Evaluate the whole system rather than celebrating salary alone. A job with coverage may solve a bridge problem more efficiently than equivalent cash, while variable income can complicate subsidy estimates.

List nonportfolio income by start date and inflation behavior: rent net of vacancy and repairs, royalties, pensions, Social Security, annuities and part-time earnings. Do not capitalise uncertain income as though it were guaranteed. Track concentration and counterparty risk.

SSA states that benefit amounts depend on earnings history and claiming age and provides personalized comparisons. Using the official estimate allows the model to show portfolio spending before and after benefits begin. The claiming decision involves longevity, household needs, work and tax—not merely a breakeven age.

Consider a phased exit: reduce hours, take a sabbatical, test a lower-cost location or fund one year from cash while employment remains available. A trial reveals the true budget, daily-life fit and insurance mechanics. It can expose missing assumptions before the resignation becomes hard to reverse.

Define return-to-work triggers without shame. Examples include reserve below a floor, funded ratio below a band, benefit loss or an essential expense increase. Also define acceptable work: maximum hours, travel, minimum net pay and health constraints. The contingency should preserve autonomy rather than default to any job.

Retirement is a change in how time is allocated, not a purity test about earned income. Optional work, delayed retirement or a higher savings target can all be rational responses to a longer horizon. The plan should maximize durable choice, not conformity to an online label.

Value unpaid benefits of employment before leaving: disability coverage, life insurance, retirement-plan match, subsidized care, professional licensing and paid leave. Replacing them can raise the true cost of FIRE even when take-home spending appears unchanged.

Account Access and Tax Rules Can Break the Bridge

A household can be wealthy on paper and short of accessible cash. Inventory every account by tax treatment, withdrawal rules, penalties, settlement time and owner. Then map which account funds each bridge year. Do this before leaving employment, when plan options and payroll contributions may be easier to change.

In the United States, the IRS generally imposes an additional tax on certain early retirement-plan distributions but lists exceptions, including substantially equal periodic payments and some distributions after separation from service at specified ages. The IRS table also shows that exceptions differ between qualified plans and IRAs. Do not assume one rule applies to every account.

Some access strategies are rigid and mistakes can trigger tax or recapture consequences. Substantially equal periodic payments, conversions and plan-specific separation rules need current professional review. An internet flowchart is not adequate support for a multi-year legal and tax election.

Preserve an accessible buffer for tax estimates and ordinary timing. Investment sales, bank transfers and benefit payments do not all settle on the day a bill is due. Quarterly tax obligations or annual reconciliations can create lumpy outflows even when annual totals appear funded.

Build a tax-year withdrawal plan across taxable, tax-deferred and tax-free accounts, but avoid universal sequencing claims. The best order can depend on brackets, gains, credits, required distributions, estate goals and future benefits. Coordinate the current year with later years rather than minimizing one tax bill in isolation.

Marketplace healthcare assistance can depend on household income, making conversions, gains and withdrawals relevant to premiums and credits. HealthCare.gov says assistance depends on income and household size. That linkage means investment and health decisions cannot be modeled in separate spreadsheets.

Maintain cost basis, contribution records and benefit statements. Confirm that former employer plans have current contact details and beneficiary designations. A clean account inventory reduces the chance that a transfer, rollover or missing record delays money needed for the bridge.

Protect access against fraud with unique credentials, multifactor authentication and alerts, but do not store passwords in the retirement plan. Record institutions, account purpose and trusted contacts securely. The plan should remain usable during travel, illness or incapacity without weakening security.

Stress a year in which access is delayed or a tax estimate is wrong. The emergency reserve should cover operational disruption without forcing an unsuitable sale. Liquidity is not merely an asset class; it is the ability to meet obligations under real account rules.

Test account access with a small routine transaction well before the exit, without triggering unnecessary tax or penalty. Confirm bank links, settlement, withholding choices and contact details. An untested operational path is not dependable bridge liquidity.

Use a Go or Wait Decision Sheet

Convert the plan into gates rather than one portfolio target. Gate one: at least twelve months of reconciled spending plus irregular costs. Gate two: written coverage for health and other essential insurance. Gate three: accessible bridge funding. Gate four: portfolio and withdrawal stress tests. Gate five: household agreement and a contingency path.

Add debt and housing gates. High-rate debt or an unaffordable housing obligation can make the retirement floor fragile. Paying debt is not always mathematically superior to investing, but fixed obligations reduce flexibility. Model the balance sheet and cash flow together.

Require a current benefit record and pension statement. Confirm earnings history, estimated amounts, claiming ages, survivor choices and inflation treatment. A screenshot from years ago is not enough. The SSA recommends checking personalized estimates and the effects of different claiming ages. The official calculator should be refreshed before launch.

Test at least four cases: base, poor early markets, high inflation plus healthcare, and lower optional income. Record the action each case triggers. A simulation with an impressive probability but no operational response plan leaves the household watching risk rather than managing it.

Set minimum margins: accessible reserve, floor-coverage ratio, maximum initial portfolio draw and minimum balance after known one-time expenses. These are personal policy choices, not universal thresholds. Their purpose is to stop a desired retirement date from quietly weakening every assumption.

Include nonfinancial readiness. Partners may disagree about spending cuts, travel, caregiving, relocation or paid work. Decide who monitors investments, pays tax, handles insurance and keeps records. A technically funded plan can fail through unassigned operations or conflicting expectations.

Choose wait actions in advance. Another six or twelve months can add contributions, reduce debt, vest benefits, test expenses, secure coverage or shorten the bridge. Waiting should have a measurable purpose rather than becoming indefinite fear. State what evidence will convert wait to go.

Choose go actions too: employment notice, insurance enrollment, cash positioning, contribution changes, account access test, tax appointment and calendar of reviews. Sequence them so coverage does not lapse and money is not moved unnecessarily early.

Sign and date the decision sheet. Revisit it after a major market move or life change before the exit, but do not move the goalposts because of routine noise. A documented go decision is more robust than a celebratory balance notification.

Add a red-team review by someone who did not build the spreadsheet. Ask them to locate the healthcare assumption, identify every income start date and explain the bad-market response. If the rules cannot be found or understood, simplify the document before signing.

Retirement Math Needs an Annual Operating Cycle

Rebuild the plan each year using actual spending, current balances, updated benefits, current insurance and tax rules. Do not merely increase last year's budget and assume all else remained true. Reconcile projected withdrawals with statements and explain the difference.

Run the review in a fixed order: household changes, spending ledger, insurance, outside income, tax and account access, portfolio allocation, funded ratio, guardrails and next-year cash. The order keeps lifestyle obligations ahead of market commentary.

Update large irregular items and the home-maintenance schedule. A project delayed last year did not disappear; it moved closer. Refill sinking funds after use. Separate genuine surprises from predictable expenses that the prior plan omitted.

Review health coverage before enrollment deadlines and after income changes. Confirm networks, medication coverage, maximum exposure and eligibility. Keep notices and tax forms. Insurance is an annual contract decision, not a one-time checkbox at retirement.

Apply the withdrawal rule mechanically, then discuss whether life changes justify a policy amendment. Calculate inflation adjustment, current withdrawal percentage, guardrail status and floor coverage. If spending changes, record whether it is a temporary response or a new lasting baseline.

Rebalance according to the investment policy rather than raising risk to repair a funding gap. If the plan deteriorates, the response can combine spending, income, goal, housing and benefit choices. A more aggressive portfolio is not a guaranteed solution and can deepen sequence damage.

Maintain a dashboard with only decision-useful numbers: essential annual spending, full annual spending, accessible reserve, twelve-month withdrawal, portfolio value, funded ratio, later-income dates and guardrail status. More charts do not create more control.

Schedule a deeper professional review when tax law, residency, health, estate, pension or access strategies change. Advice is most valuable where mistakes are hard to reverse. Retain assumptions and questions so the meeting tests the plan instead of starting from scattered account statements.

FIRE did not become impossible because one withdrawal-rate estimate changed. It became more honest when the plan stopped pretending one multiplier could represent a lifetime. Track the expense layers, bridge years, access rules, income dates, sequence response and annual decisions. Independence rests on that operating system—not one number.

Archive each annual version rather than overwriting history. Comparing forecast with actual shows persistent bias in travel, healthcare, tax or maintenance. The error pattern is more useful than defending last year's assumptions and steadily improves the next decision.

The FIRE decision in brief

A FIRE target is a scenario, not proof that retirement is funded. Multiplying annual spending by 25 assumes a 4% starting rate, but changing the rate to 3.5% or 3% materially changes the target. Every result still depends on duration, portfolio, inflation, taxes, spending flexibility and later income.

Build spending from at least twelve months of actual transactions. Separate essential, committed, discretionary and irregular costs. Add housing maintenance, healthcare, tax, replacements and known one-time expenses. Create both a floor budget and a full-life budget so any claimed flexibility can be measured in money.

Price the bridge years explicitly. Early retirement can begin long before public health eligibility, Social Security or unrestricted retirement-account access. Obtain real insurance quotes, update official benefit estimates with the intended stop-work age and map portfolio withdrawals year by year until later income begins.

A withdrawal rate must state its horizon, portfolio, inflation rule, tax treatment and success definition. A thirty-year study does not automatically answer a fifty-year plan. Test several horizons and define success using reserves, survivor needs, care or legacy rather than merely a positive final balance.

Sequence risk matters because poor returns early in retirement combine with withdrawals and leave fewer assets for recovery. Stress the first five and ten years using market declines, inflation and irregular expenses. Measure the actual discretionary spending available for guardrail cuts instead of assuming all spending can flex.

Optional income is a risk control, not a failure. Model it conservatively after taxes and work costs, and do not make the base plan depend on employment that may not be available. A phased exit or trial year can verify spending, coverage and daily-life assumptions before a permanent resignation.

Inventory accounts by tax treatment, access rules and penalties. Map which assets fund each bridge year and obtain current advice before using rigid early-access strategies. Coordinate withdrawals with healthcare assistance, capital gains, conversions, future benefits and tax rather than minimizing one year's bill alone.

Use a go-or-wait sheet with separate gates for reconciled spending, insurance, accessible bridge assets, stress-tested withdrawals, debt, housing, updated benefits and household agreement. Define what another year of work would accomplish and what evidence permits launch.

Operate the plan annually. Reconcile spending, update irregular costs, renew insurance assumptions, verify outside income, apply guardrails, review access and tax, and rebalance under policy. Track essential spending, full spending, reserve, withdrawal, portfolio value, funded ratio and later-income dates.

Financial independence is durable when essential life is funded, shocks can be absorbed and discretionary choices can respond without panic. The useful math is a system of expense layers, bridge periods, access rules, withdrawal responses and annual decisions—not a single portfolio milestone.

Run a practice version before resigning. Live for several months on the proposed full budget, route the rest to savings and record where the plan breaks. Price employment benefits that disappear, including insurance, disability coverage, retirement matching, paid leave and professional costs that may become personal expenses.

Use sensitivity tables instead of searching for one perfect forecast. Change retirement date, spending, inflation, early returns and later income separately, then combine unfavorable cases. The assumptions that most change the decision require the best evidence, strongest contingency and widest margin.

Test operations as well as arithmetic. Confirm bank links, settlement time, withholding, insurance enrollment dates and trusted access before leaving work. Retain each annual model and compare forecast with actual so repeated errors in healthcare, tax, travel or maintenance improve the next review. A plan that cannot move money, maintain coverage or explain its response under pressure is not ready, regardless of the portfolio balance shown on that particular day.

Before leaving work

Is annual spending multiplied by 25 a complete FIRE target?

No. It is an orientation based on a 4% starting rate and omits horizon, taxes, healthcare, access, later income and spending flexibility.

How many years of spending should be reviewed?

Use at least twelve reconciled months and add irregular costs, replacements and known changes after leaving work.

Why do bridge years matter?

Portfolio withdrawals may begin years before public benefits, health eligibility or penalty-free access to particular accounts.

Can part-time work be included?

Yes, but the base plan should not depend on uncertain work. Model net income after tax and costs and treat optional earnings as a contingency.

What is sequence-of-returns risk?

Poor early returns combined with withdrawals can leave fewer assets to benefit from a later recovery, even when long-run average returns look adequate.

How often should the FIRE plan be updated?

Reconcile it at least annually and after major life, health, residency, tax or benefit changes.

Retirement planning terms

FIRE
Financial independence, retire early; a goal of funding life without dependence on full-time employment.
Initial withdrawal rate
First-year portfolio withdrawal divided by the starting portfolio value.
Sequence risk
The effect that the order of investment returns has when withdrawals occur.
Bridge years
Years between leaving work and the start of later benefits or account access.
Floor budget
Essential spending that cannot readily be reduced during market stress.
Guardrail
A predefined threshold that changes withdrawals or other actions.
Funded ratio
Current investable assets divided by the updated assets required under the plan.
Longevity risk
The risk of outliving available assets and income.
Sinking fund
Money accumulated gradually for a known irregular future expense.
Dynamic spending
A withdrawal approach that changes spending under stated portfolio or market rules.
